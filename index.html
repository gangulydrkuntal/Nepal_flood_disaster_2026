Geospatial Reconstruction · Remote Measurement

Anatomy of the Nepal Flood due to Langtang Lirung glacier collapse.

By Dr. Kuntal Ganguly, Earth Observation and Geospatial Architect,
Cognizant Worldwide Ltd., London, United Kingdom

On 26 August 2026, a massive ice–rock failure involving a hanging glacier below Tsangbu Ri (near Langtang Lirung along the Nepal–Tibet border) collapsed into the Lhende Khola valley. This unleashed a violent debris flow and flash flood that swept past the Gyirong/Rasuwagadhi border post and surged down the Bhote Koshi–Trishuli corridor for nearly 100 km. This study deeply analyses the disaster from two distinct perspectives. First, we investigate the geomorphological triggers and the mechanics behind the massive kinetic energy from the initial collapse. Second, we trace the history of human activities, infrastructure development, and settlement patterns along this vulnerable river corridor since 2005. By comparing pre-event records with newly released, post-disaster high-resolution satellite imagery made available to the public recently, this analysis maps the precise footprint of the surge to evaluate the true scale of the impact and downstream losses.

Where

The source zone sits on a hanging glacier beneath the Tsangbu Ri massif, straddling the Nepal–China border. Meltwater and debris followed the Bhote Koshi drainage southward past Khangjim and Thuman, joining the Trishuli valley toward Syabrubesi — the same high-relief corridor connecting the Kyirong (Gyirong) border crossing to the Langtang region.

PHASE 01

The source zone

Before it failed, the glacier covered roughly 4.6 km² below Tsangbu Ri — a compact but thick ice body feeding the Bhote Koshi headwaters. Cross-referencing several sources, the section that ultimately broke away is estimated at around 0.2 km²: a small fraction of the total mass, but positioned on the steepest part of the icefall.

PHASE 02

Initial failure — the first 2.6 km

The detached ice mass let go on a headwall pitched between 62° and 67° — steep enough that the initial descent behaved more like a rockfall than a glide. In a single continuous drop it fell from about 17,058 ft to 12,110 ft, covering an estimated 2.5–2.9 km of horizontal distance before the slope eased.

PHASE 03

Valley runout — measured 30 km, and continuing

Past the initial icefall, debris and meltwater entered a far gentler channel — a median slope of just 2.1° — and rode the Bhote Koshi–Trishuli drainage past Khangjim and Thuman toward Syabrubesi, losing the remaining 7,200 ft of elevation gradually. The path was traced to roughly 30 km, which is where this measurement stops rather than where the debris stopped: the material continued downstream beyond the extent captured here.

Exposure context

What sits in the runout path

No post-event imagery is included in this reconstruction. Instead, a pre-disaster 3D flythrough built from two decades of Google Earth historical imagery was reviewed to understand what has changed along the Bhote Koshi–Trishuli corridor. It shows steady infrastructure build-out running the full length of the drainage, upstream near the border down to the valley towns below — the same corridor this debris travelled.

That build-out matters here only as context: a corridor that was comparatively undeveloped two decades ago now carries considerably more roads, settlement, and fixed infrastructure directly beneath a known icefall.

How this analysis was built

METHOD · BHOTE KOSHI – TRISHULI CORRIDOR

Observation is bounded by what the public imagery could see — cloud cover, not damage, sets the limits of this dataset.

01

Imagery availability

Public post-disaster high-resolution imagery released for the affected districts was catalogued to establish what could actually be observed.

02

Cloud-free screening

Every candidate scene along the Bhote Koshi and Trishuli was checked and only cloud-free footprints were retained as valid observation windows.

03

Before / after pairing

Each usable location was paired with a February 2026 baseline scene and its 27–28 August 2026 post-event counterpart at matched scale.

04

Building-level counting

Structures within the flood- and debris-affected extent were counted per site and geo-referenced to a single coordinate.

Result: 14 cloud-free locations across ~40 km of the corridor, from the Nepal–China border down to Nuwakot, with 1,317 buildings assessed as impacted.

Reviewing upstream to downstream corridor

REVIEWING · SITES 01–14

Three distinct damage regimes emerge along the ~40 km assessed reach.

Upper Bhote Koshi
Sites 01–07 · border to Syapru Besi
699 buildings 53% of total

The surge enters at the Nepal–China border, taking out the checkpoint approach and an under-construction hydel site with limited built-up loss, then intensifies along NH 42 where roadside hotels, dense settlement (342 buildings) and two bridges are stripped out. Syapru Besi is the first townships-scale loss at 208 buildings.

Mid corridor
Sites 08–10 · confluence reach
28 buildings 2% of total

Through the narrow confluence reach, the valley is steep and sparsely built. Damage drops to a handful of riverside structures and an under-construction site — the flood is destructive but there is little in its path.

Lower Trishuli
Sites 11–14 · Betrawati to Nuwakot
590 buildings 45% of total

Where the valley widens, the river spreads across settled floodplain. Betrawati is the single worst-hit location at 367 buildings, followed by the Trishuli dam settlements and Nuwakot, with a bridge crossing lost in between.

Counts are conservative — cloud-obscured stretches could not be assessed.
